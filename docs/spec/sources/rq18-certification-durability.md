# RQ18 — Certification Durability Under Drift and Change

## TL;DR
- **There is no defensible universal certificate lifetime.** A Core certificate should be bound to a content-addressed "certified context hash" over its entire dependency closure and treated as *void the instant any element of that closure changes* (event-driven invalidation), with a short TTL (hours-to-weeks for model-dependent gates) acting only as a fail-closed backstop for changes you cannot observe — chiefly silent provider-side model updates, which are demonstrably real (Model Equality Testing found 11 of 31 commercial Llama endpoints served distributions differing from Meta's reference weights; GPT-4's prime-identification accuracy fell from 84.0% to 51.1% in three months).
- **The correct architecture is hybrid, tiered, and content-addressed:** mechanical hard-invalidation on any hash change in the closure; continuous *anytime-valid* behavioral monitoring (conformal test martingales / e-processes) to catch unannounced drift without false-alarm inflation; and cascaded recertification (cheap screen → scoped differential recert → full recert) prioritized by the lexicographic harm tier. This mirrors mature practice in TLS (47-day certs by 2029), Sigstore (10-minute certs), FDA PCCPs, ISO 26262 SEooC assumptions-of-use, and Bazel/Nix Merkle-DAG cache invalidation.
- **Assurance cost is minimized not by lengthening lifetimes but by making invalidation mechanical and recertification differential** — re-run only the gates whose inputs changed, reuse prior evidence under assume-guarantee contracts, and screen cheaply before spending eval-sample budget. The dominant failure mode to design against is not over-invalidation but *stale certification silently authorizing materially changed behavior*, so all defaults must fail closed.

## Key Findings

### The literature is asymmetrically mature
Every discipline surveyed has independently converged on the same core insight: **long-lived assurance artifacts are a liability, and the reliable fix is short lifetimes plus automation, not better revocation.** What is genuinely thin is any published treatment of *composed, transitive* certificate invalidation for agentic AI systems specifically — that part is my synthesis, extrapolated from build systems and supply-chain provenance. The "how long" question has no published universal answer in any field; every field instead ties validity to *change events* and uses time only as a backstop.

### Change is not one thing — invalidation semantics differ by axis
The eight drift axes in the question have genuinely different invalidation semantics and must be treated separately. Model/serving changes and policy changes are the most dangerous because they can be *silent* (no version identifier changes) and *materially behavior-altering*, respectively.

## Details

### 1. How existing disciplines handle certificate/assurance expiry

**ML/AI monitoring and drift detection (established practice).** The field distinguishes covariate shift (P(X) changes), concept drift (P(Y|X) changes), prior/label shift (P(Y) changes), each requiring different detection. Workhorse statistics: **Population Stability Index (PSI)** with the industry-standard thresholds <0.10 stable, 0.10–0.25 investigate, >0.25 retrain (originated in credit scorecard monitoring); **Kolmogorov–Smirnov** two-sample test (hypersensitive at scale — pair with an effect-size threshold); **chi-squared** for categoricals; streaming detectors **ADWIN** and **Page–Hinkley**; and CUSUM. The critical asymmetry: covariate drift is detectable immediately from inputs, but concept drift requires ground-truth labels that arrive with lag (days to months), so it is often detected only after performance has degraded.

**The silent-model-update problem (established, with hard numbers).** Chen, Zaharia & Zou ("How Is ChatGPT's Behavior Changing over Time?", arXiv:2307.09009; Harvard Data Science Review 6(2), 2024) showed the "same" LLM service changes substantially in months: GPT-4's prime/composite accuracy dropped from 84.0% (March 2023) to 51.1% (June 2023) while verbosity collapsed from 638.3 to 3.9 characters; GPT-3.5 moved the opposite direction (49.6%→76.2%). (Note the widely-cited 97.6%→2.4% figure is from the superseded arXiv v1 and was revised after methodological critique; use 84.0%→51.1%.) Their earlier "How Did the Model Change?" (ICLR 2022) built MASA, a UCB-based sampling method to assess API confusion-matrix shifts under a sample budget, finding significant shifts in 12 of 36 commercial API applications from 2020–2021.

Detecting silent changes has matured into named black-box methods: **Model Equality Testing** (Gao, Liang & Guestrin, ICLR 2025, arXiv:2410.20247) frames it as a two-sample test using **Maximum Mean Discrepancy with a Hamming-distance string kernel**, achieving median 77.4% power with ~10 samples/prompt at <$1/audit, and found **11 of 31 commercial Llama endpoints served distributions differing from Meta's reference weights**. **B3IT** ("Token-Efficient Change Detection in LLM APIs", Chauvin et al., arXiv:2602.11083, ICML 2026) uses "border inputs" (prompts where two top tokens are nearly tied at low temperature, becoming extremely sensitive detectors) for a 30× cost reduction over MET in strict black-box settings, monitoring 93 endpoints. **Behavioral fingerprinting** (Stability Monitor, arXiv:2603.19022) samples a fixed prompt set periodically, compares output-embedding distributions via a summed **energy-distance** statistic with permutation-test p-values aggregated sequentially, and in the wild detected an infrastructure change at provider Parasail traced to a GPU hardware migration — demonstrating that "same model, different behavior" is real even absent any version change.

**Eval staleness and contamination (established).** Benchmark data contamination inflates scores through memorization: MMLU contamination tripled from 9% to 29.4% in three years (December 2020 vs. October 2023), per Li et al. (2024), "LLM Benchmark Datasets Should Be Contamination-Resistant" (arXiv:2605.19999, Fig. 1); the 29.1% cross-sectional figure and a parallel C-Eval rate of 45.8% are from Deng et al., "Investigating Data Contamination in Modern Benchmarks for LLMs," NAACL 2024 (arXiv:2311.09783). A clean-mirror re-test dropped Mistral's GSM8K by up to 8 points in the peer-reviewed version of Zhang et al. (Scale AI), "A Careful Examination of Large Language Model Performance on Grade School Arithmetic" (arXiv:2405.00332, NeurIPS 2024) — which found the Phi and Mistral families "showing evidence of systematic overfitting" (the widely-circulated "up to 13%" figure is from arXiv v1; cite up to 8pt). OpenAI's Frontier Evals team ("Why SWE-bench Verified no longer measures frontier model performance," Feb 23, 2026) reported that "all frontier models we tested were able to reproduce the original, human-written bug fix used as the ground-truth reference, known as the gold patch, or verbatim problem statement specifics for certain tasks" (tested models included GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5, and Gemini 3 Flash), and now reports SWE-bench Pro instead. Mitigations: private held-out test servers (GLUE/SuperGLUE/MMLU-CF), dynamic/rotating benchmarks (LiveBench refreshes monthly), and canary strings. LLM-as-judge reliability is itself unstable (Yamauchi et al., GEM 2026) and drifts as the judge model updates — a reference-protocol-drift hazard directly relevant to foreign verifiers.

**Anytime-valid monitoring (established, and the key enabling technique).** Naive continuous monitoring inflates false positives — with unlimited peeking, Type-I error is guaranteed (Johari et al., "Always Valid Inference", Operations Research 2022). The fix is **safe anytime-valid inference (SAVI)**: e-processes, test martingales, and confidence sequences that remain valid at *any* stopping time (Ramdas, Grünwald, Vovk & Shafer, Statistical Science 2023), grounded in Ville's inequality and "testing by betting." Conformal test martingales apply this to drift (conformal martingale ensembles for concept drift). Applied to deployment monitoring: **WATCH** (Prinster, Han, Liu & Saria, ICML 2025) uses weighted-conformal test martingales for continual, anytime-valid changepoint monitoring with provable false-alarm control via Ville's inequality, adapting online to benign covariate shift while flagging harmful concept shift. This is the statistical foundation that lets Core monitor certificates continuously without recertification-triggering false alarms.

**Formal methods (established; strong analogy for differential recert).** Proof reuse and incremental/regression verification only re-verify what changed: incremental verification reuses refinement constraints or trace abstractions across program versions; IBM's incremental formal verification reuses invariants (invars) from a prior proof as candidate invariants for the modified model, checking their continued validity rather than re-proving from scratch. **Proof-carrying code** ships a checkable certificate so verification reduces to cheap certificate-checking vs. expensive re-proving — directly analogous to Core verifying a certificate's context hash rather than re-running the gate. **Assume-guarantee / contract-based design** (Benveniste et al., "Contracts for System Design") is the central reuse mechanism: a component's guarantee holds *only while its assumptions hold*; compositional NN verification (CoVeNN) and code-level ADAS verification (CoCoSaFe) show verified subsystems can be reused without re-verification *as long as their assumptions remain valid*. The invalidation rule falls out mechanically: a certificate is valid iff its assumptions still hold in the new context.

**Safety engineering (established, regulatory-grade).**
- **FDA Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCP)** — finalized Aug 2025, grounded in FD&C Act §515C — pre-authorize *specified* future AI/ML modifications via three components: a description of modifications (with boundaries, triggers, abort conditions), a modification protocol (validation datasets, statistical methods, acceptance criteria, rollback), and an impact assessment. Changes outside the PCCP require a new submission. 53 devices had FDA-authorized PCCPs by end of 2024 (up from ~35 mid-2024), per Choudhury et al. (PMC12577744), citing B. O'Leary's PCCP database: "Up until the end of 2024, a total of 1,016 AI-ML-enabled medical devices have been approved by the FDA, 53 devices with PCCPs." This is the closest regulatory analogue to Core's "pre-declared change envelope."
- **ISO 26262 SEooC** (Safety Element out of Context): a component is certified against explicit **Assumptions of Use (AoU)**; at integration the integrator must validate each assumption as valid/partially-valid/invalid, and an invalid assumption triggers redesign, supplier modification, additional analysis, or rejection. Crucially: "an SEooC with a TÜV certificate is not automatically safe in every application" — the certificate confirms development against *stated assumptions*, not their validity in context. Plus proven-in-use and the modification lifecycle with impact analysis.
- **DO-178C** uses Design Assurance Levels (DAL A–E, 71 objectives at DAL A) and change-impact analysis for reuse; **IEC 61508** has a modification lifecycle requiring impact analysis before change.
- **Dynamic safety cases** (Denney, Pai & Habli, ICSE 2015) operationalize through-life assurance: a safety argument annotated with metadata, a confidence structure, monitors linking system to argument, and *update rules* triggering actions when observations breach thresholds or confidence in a claim drops. The failure mode this addresses is **safety-case erosion / assurance deficit** — the argument silently decaying as the world changes. GSN with confidence arguments is the notation (3/4 of UK military aircraft used GSN-based safety cases as of 2014).

**Security & supply chain (established; strongest case for short lifetimes).** The TLS ecosystem is the canonical proof that short lifetimes beat revocation: CA/Browser Forum ballot SC-081v3 (passed 25–0 among CAs, April 2025) drops max TLS cert lifetime from 398 days → 200 (Mar 2026) → 100 (Mar 2027) → **47 days (Mar 2029)**, with domain-validation reuse dropping to 10 days. The stated rationale: certificate information "is becoming steadily less trustworthy over time," and CRL/OCSP revocation is unreliable (soft-fail OCSP lets a network attacker bypass it), so short lifetimes are "the blunt but reliable alternative." **Sigstore/Fulcio** takes this to the limit: **10-minute** code-signing certificates, eliminating revocation entirely — "the user only needs to know that the artifact was signed while the certificate was valid," with Rekor's transparency log providing the timestamp. **SLSA provenance / in-toto attestations** bind claims to immutable **subject digests** ("Subject artifacts are matched purely by digest") with `resolvedDependencies` capturing the transitive closure for recursive supply-chain analysis; the `predicateType` URI carries a major version that changes on any backward-incompatible change. **SBOM/VEX** data "may quickly get stale." **NIST RMF / FedRAMP** shifted from static 3-year ATO reauthorization toward **ongoing authorization** — continuous monitoring supporting a persistent, time-or-event-driven decision, where "reauthorizations are unnecessary in situations where the continuous monitoring program provides authorizing officials with the information necessary to manage risk," and an event producing risk above tolerance (breach, or *failure of the continuous-monitoring program itself*) triggers reauthorization.

**Statistics/experimental design (established).** Preregistration invalidation is nuanced: not all deviations are fatal (Lakens 2024) — some *increase* validity — but they must be declared and their impact on test severity evaluated; substantial deviations force re-review. Clinical prediction models show **calibration decay** in non-stationary environments (an Epic sepsis RNN degraded 0.729→0.525 AUC over a decade, partly from the ICD-9→ICD-10 coding change — a schema-drift example). **Temporal validation** and **temporal recalibration** update the baseline hazard on recent data while holding predictor effects; calibration-drift detection uses adaptive sliding windows + online SGD to trigger updates data-driven rather than on fixed schedules ("fixed updating... [is] inefficient and sometimes even detrimental"). SPRT, alpha-spending, and e-processes handle repeated testing without error inflation.

**Systems/caching (established; the mechanical model for Core).** Bazel defines each action by its command line, environment, and **input file hashes**; the action cache maps action hashes → results over a content-addressable store — change any input and the action hash changes, forcing re-execution. **Nix** derivations are input-addressed: the output path hashes the *recipe*; anything missing from the derivation breaks reproducibility (Zakaria's "the Nix sandbox is a hidden input" shows the danger of an unhashed dependency — directly analogous to an unhashed provider-side model version). **Merkle-DAG invalidation** (as in Bazel/Make and DAG cohort caches): each node's hash incorporates its dependencies' hashes, so "a change anywhere automatically produces different hashes for all downstream nodes. No explicit invalidation logic is needed." This is exactly the property Core should adopt: make invalidation *mechanical, not judgment-based*.

### 2. Synthesis: a concrete strategy for Core

#### 2a. The certified context hash
Every Core certificate MUST embed a **certified context hash (CCH)** — a BLAKE3 content-addressed digest over its entire dependency closure, computed Merkle-style so invalidation propagates automatically. The CCH MUST cover, at minimum:
- **Model identity + served version** — provider, model family, published version string, *and* a behavioral fingerprint hash (see 2c) to catch silent server-side changes where no version string changes;
- **Prompt/system-prompt hash** (all prompt templates and system prompts);
- **Tool versions** (every tool's version + interface schema hash, including external API contract versions);
- **Schema/data-contract versions** (input/output schemas, data contracts);
- **Policy version** (safety policy, authorization-monitor config, harm-hierarchy version);
- **Eval/reference-protocol version** (the verification protocol, oracle, and any LLM-judge model+prompt — because the judge itself drifts);
- **Dependency certificate hashes** (the CCH of every certificate this one composes, giving transitive closure).

Given the CCH, invalidation is mechanical: recompute the closure hash; if it differs, the certificate is void. This removes human judgment from the invalidation decision — the hardest failure mode (judgment-based invalidation that quietly never fires) is designed out.

**The hard case: provider-side model changes with no version identifier.** This is where content-addressing alone fails, because the input that changed is not exposed. Core cannot hash what the provider will not reveal. The only sound answers are (i) include a **behavioral fingerprint** in the CCH and refresh it continuously, treating a fingerprint shift as a hash change; (ii) apply a **short TTL backstop** so an undetected silent change ages out; and (iii) **fail closed** — treat "model served version unverifiable" as an unknown dependency state that degrades the certificate to void, not valid. Self-hosted or digest-pinned weights are the only way to fully close this gap; API-served models are irreducibly provisional.

#### 2b. Taxonomy of change classes → invalidation action (decision table)

| Change class | Default invalidation | Recert scope | Rationale / analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Model weights/version (announced)** | **Hard-void** | Full recert of model-dependent gates; reuse gates provably independent of the model under A/G contracts | Behavior can change arbitrarily (Chen et al.); Bazel input-hash change |
| **Model serving stack / silent update** (quantization, kernel, routing, hardware) | **Soft-invalidate → provisional**; hard-void on fingerprint-shift detection | Cheap fingerprint screen → scoped recert if shift confirmed | Stability Monitor caught quantization/GPU-migration shifts; "same model, different behavior" |
| **Prompt / system-prompt** | **Hard-void** | Differential: only gates exercising the changed prompt path | Prompt is a hashed input; small changes alter behavior |
| **Tool version / external API drift** | **Hard-void** if interface/behavior changed; intact if pinned & unchanged | Scoped to gates invoking that tool + integration gates | A/G: tool guarantee is an assumption of the composed cert |
| **Schema / data contract** | **Hard-void** | Gates consuming/producing the schema; downstream transitively | Schema drift caused Epic sepsis AUC decay; in-toto subject-digest |
| **Policy (safety/authz/harm hierarchy)** | **Hard-void immediately, no grace period** | Full re-evaluation against new policy for all affected tiers | Policy defines the pass criterion itself; fail-closed on the harm hierarchy |
| **Task/input distribution shift** | **Soft-invalidate → provisional** (certificate was scoped to a distribution) | Condition-based: recert triggered by anytime-valid monitor breach | Covariate/concept drift; certificate's AoU is the input distribution |
| **Reference/eval protocol (incl. judge model)** | **Hard-void** | Re-run affected gates under new protocol; re-baseline | Judge drift; preregistration-protocol change; refute-by-default |
| **Dependency certificate (transitive)** | **Hard-void (propagated)** | Only the transitively affected gates | Merkle propagation; SLSA resolvedDependencies |

"Hard-void" = certificate immediately invalid, no authorization. "Soft-invalidate → provisional" = certificate degrades to a provisional state that authorizes only under additional runtime guards (or not at all for high harm tiers) pending a cheap re-check. The distinction between hard and soft should itself be *raised* by harm tier: for the top harm tier, treat every soft-invalidate as a hard-void (fail closed).

#### 2c. TTL vs. event-driven vs. hybrid
- **Event-driven (content-addressed) invalidation dominates** whenever the changed input is observable and hashable — it is exact, immediate, and mechanical (no false invalidations, no missed real changes). This should be the primary mechanism.
- **TTL is necessary but secondary**, justified *only* for the unobservable-change residue — chiefly silent provider-side model updates and slow distribution shift. The TLS/Sigstore argument applies directly: when you cannot reliably detect or revoke, a short lifetime is the reliable backstop. The counterargument (recertification cost) is real but is answered by *cheap tiered screening* (2e), not by lengthening TTLs.
- **Therefore: hybrid.** Event-driven as primary; continuous anytime-valid monitoring as the detector for semi-observable change (fingerprints, production SPC); short TTL as the final fail-closed backstop for the truly unobservable. TTL length should scale *inversely* with harm tier and with dependence on opaque external components.

#### 2d. Detecting unannounced change
- **Held-out sentinel/canary sets** run continuously against the live configuration; a score drop beyond an anytime-valid threshold flags drift. Rotate/keep private to avoid contamination.
- **Behavioral fingerprinting** (fixed prompt set → output-distribution distance via energy distance/MMD; B3IT border inputs for token efficiency) folded into the CCH so a fingerprint shift *is* a hash change.
- **Statistical process control on production outcomes** (PSI/KS on inputs and outputs, with effect-size gating).
- **Anytime-valid monitoring (WATCH-style weighted-conformal test martingales / e-processes)** so continuous checking does not inflate false alarms — this is what makes 24/7 monitoring compatible with a low false-invalidation rate.

#### 2e. Cost-minimizing recertification policy
- **Tiered/cascaded recert:** cheap screen (fingerprint + sentinel eval, ~10 samples/prompt à la MET, <$1) → scoped differential recert of only affected gates → full recert only if the screen fails or harm tier demands it.
- **Risk-based prioritization by harm tier:** allocate eval-sample budget and human review lexicographically by the harm hierarchy; top-tier gates get full recert on any soft signal, low-tier gates ride monitoring.
- **Differential/regression recert:** re-run only gates whose CCH inputs changed (Merkle-scoped), reusing all others' evidence under A/G contracts — the proof-reuse / incremental-verification analogue.
- **Evidence reuse under A/G contracts:** a gate's prior pass is reusable iff its assumptions (its slice of the CCH) are unchanged.
- **Screening threshold:** set via the anytime-valid framework to bound false-invalidation rate at a chosen α while retaining power; use condition-based rather than fixed-schedule triggering (clinical calibration-drift evidence shows fixed schedules are inefficient/detrimental).
- **Formal framing:** the recertify-vs-wait decision is a **condition-based maintenance / POMDP** problem — the certificate's true validity is partially observable, monitors give noisy signals, and optimal policy trades inspection cost against the risk of authorizing stale behavior. Concretely, Guo & Liang ("A predictive Markov decision process for optimizing inspection and maintenance strategies of partially observable multi-state systems," *Reliability Engineering & System Safety*, 2022, DOI S0951832022003167) report a PMDP that "outperforms the POMDP under routine inspection by saving 26.3% of the cost on average." Age-based renewal (TTL) is the special case when no condition signal exists.

#### 2f. Spec invariants (fail-closed)
1. **No certificate may authorize behavior outside its certified context hash.** Any request whose resolved context hash ≠ the certificate's CCH is unauthorized.
2. **Unknown/unverifiable dependency state fails closed.** If any closure element cannot be resolved or verified (e.g., provider will not confirm served version), the certificate is void, not valid.
3. **Transitive invalidation must propagate.** Voiding any certificate voids all certificates whose CCH includes it (Merkle propagation).
4. **A certificate is never self-renewing.** Renewal requires a fresh verification act producing a new certificate with a new CCH and issuance timestamp; TTL extension by the certificate itself is prohibited.
5. **Foreign-verifier evidence is refuted by default** and only credited when the verifier's own certificate (including its judge-model/protocol version) is currently valid.

#### 2g. Metrics
- **Certificate staleness:** wall-clock since issuance ÷ TTL, *and* count of closure elements whose live hash is unconfirmed.
- **Assurance debt:** aggregate risk-weighted count of provisional/expired certificates still gating live behavior (weight by harm tier).
- **Time-to-detect-invalidating-change (TTD):** latency from a real change to invalidation — measured against injected canary changes.
- **False-invalidation rate:** fraction of invalidations that scoped recert shows were unnecessary (the anytime-valid α bounds this).
- **Recertification cost per unit assurance:** compute + wall-clock + human-minutes + eval-samples per gate recertified, per harm-tier-weighted coverage restored.
- **Certified-context coverage:** fraction of the actual runtime dependency surface that is actually in the CCH (measures the silent-update blind spot).

#### 2h. Failure modes & anti-patterns
- **Rubber-stamp recert** — recert that re-issues without re-running gates; defeated by requiring a fresh verification act (invariant 4) and logging eval-sample counts.
- **Certificate laundering through composition** — composing low-assurance certs into a high-assurance claim; defeated by transitive CCH inclusion and by capping a composite's assurance at the min of its dependencies.
- **TTL theater** — long TTLs that make expiry meaningless; defeated by tying TTL to harm tier and to opaque-dependency exposure.
- **Drift detectors that never fire** — miscalibrated or contaminated sentinels; defeated by injected-canary TTD measurement and sentinel rotation.
- **Grandfathered certificates** — legacy certs exempted from new policy; forbidden by invariant 1 (policy version is in the CCH, so a policy change hard-voids them).
- **Over-invalidation → recertification fatigue → gate bypass** — the real risk of doing this badly; defeated by *differential* recert and cheap screening so the cost of doing the right thing stays low, plus anytime-valid thresholds to suppress false alarms.

### 3. Where the literature is thin or contested
- **Genuinely thin / my synthesis:** transitive, content-addressed certificate invalidation for *composed agentic AI pipelines* specifically. The mechanics are lifted from Bazel/Nix/Merkle-DAG and SLSA, and the contract logic from assume-guarantee design, but I found no published standard applying these to AI agent-loop certificates. The CCH construction, the harm-tier-indexed hard/soft distinction, and the never-self-renewing invariant are my recommendations, not established practice.
- **Contested:** the exact GPT-4 drift magnitude (v1 97.6%→2.4% vs. final 84.0%→51.1%) — use the peer-reviewed figures. The GSM8K clean-mirror drop (v1 "up to 13%" vs. peer-reviewed "up to 8%") — same caveat. Whether behavioral fingerprinting is robust over *months* and against adaptive evasion is explicitly unresolved in the source. Whether fixed schedules are ever appropriate (clinical literature says usually not; regulatory practice still uses them as backstops).
- **Established but domain-specific:** short-lived-cert economics (TLS/Sigstore), anytime-valid monitoring (SAVI/WATCH), PCCP/SEooC assumption validation, calibration-drift detection. These transfer with adaptation but none were designed for agent-loop certificates.

### 4. The literal "how long"
There is **no defensible universal number**, for the same reason the CA/Browser Forum ties validity to change-event risk rather than picking a physically meaningful constant (their 47 days is admittedly a scheduling artifact, not a safety threshold). Defensible *decision rules* instead:
- **Primary rule:** validity = "until the certified context hash changes." Under stable inputs a certificate can remain valid indefinitely; under any hashed-input change it is void immediately. This is the only rule that is both sound and cost-minimal.
- **Backstop TTL (for unobservable change only), scaled by harm tier and opaque-dependency exposure:**
  - Top harm tier + API-served (opaque) model: **hours-to-days** (fingerprint every few hours, as Stability Monitor does; align with the shortest practical monitoring cadence).
  - Mid harm tier + API-served model: **days-to-weeks**.
  - Any tier + digest-pinned / self-hosted model with full CCH coverage: TTL can be **long (quarters)** because event-driven invalidation covers the real risk; TTL is then a formality.
  - Policy/eval-protocol dependence: no independent TTL — these hard-void on version change regardless.
- **The number should fall as (a) harm tier rises, (b) dependence on opaque external components rises, and (c) the false-invalidation cost of monitoring falls.** A fixed universal lifetime is indefensible because it is simultaneously too long for opaque high-harm gates (authorizing silently-changed behavior) and needlessly short for fully-hashed low-harm gates (pure recertification waste).

## Recommendations
1. **Build the certified context hash first.** Make every certificate carry a BLAKE3 Merkle digest over model+served-version+fingerprint, prompts, tool versions, schema versions, policy version, eval-protocol version, and dependency certificate hashes. This makes invalidation mechanical and delivers most of the assurance value immediately. Benchmark to change: if you cannot enumerate the full runtime dependency surface into the CCH, your certified-context coverage metric is <1 and every gap is a silent-drift hole — fix coverage before tuning TTLs.
2. **Adopt event-driven invalidation as primary, TTL as backstop.** Hard-void on any CCH change per the decision table. Set backstop TTLs only for the unobservable residue, scaled by harm tier (hours–days for opaque high-harm; quarters for fully-hashed low-harm).
3. **Stand up continuous anytime-valid monitoring** (WATCH-style weighted-conformal test martingales / e-processes) over held-out sentinels, behavioral fingerprints, and production SPC. Fold fingerprint shifts into the CCH. This is the single highest-leverage investment for catching silent provider-side change without false-alarm-driven recertification fatigue. Threshold: bound false-invalidation rate at a chosen α via the anytime-valid framework.
4. **Make recertification tiered and differential.** Cheap screen (fingerprint + ~10-sample sentinel eval, <$1) → Merkle-scoped differential recert of only affected gates (reusing evidence under A/G contracts) → full recert only on screen failure or top harm tier. Frame the recertify-vs-wait decision as a POMDP / condition-based-maintenance problem.
5. **Encode the five fail-closed invariants in the spec** (2f), especially "unknown dependency state fails closed" and "never self-renewing." For the top harm tier, collapse soft-invalidate into hard-void.
6. **Instrument the eight metrics** (2g), and specifically run **injected-canary TTD tests** so you can prove your drift detectors actually fire.
7. **Prefer digest-pinned / self-hosted models for high-harm gates.** API-served models are irreducibly provisional because you cannot hash what the provider will not reveal; treat any such certificate as provisional by construction.

### Thresholds that change these recommendations
- If behavioral fingerprinting proves *unstable over months / evadable* (open question in the source), demote it from a CCH input to a coarse alarm and shorten opaque-model TTLs further.
- If providers begin publishing verifiable served-version attestations (in-toto/SLSA-style), you can promote API-served certificates from provisional to hashed and lengthen their TTLs.
- If recertification cost per unit assurance stays high despite differential recert, the fix is cheaper screening or coarser gate decomposition — never longer TTLs.

## Caveats
- The transitive certificate-invalidation architecture is synthesis, not established standard; validate it against a pilot before spec-freezing.
- All quantitative drift figures are from specific studies at specific times; magnitudes are illustrative of *variance*, not stable constants. Use 84.0%→51.1% (peer-reviewed) not the withdrawn 97.6%→2.4%, and "up to 8%" not "up to 13%" for the GSM8K clean-mirror drop.
- KS and other tests are hypersensitive at production scale — always pair statistical significance with an effect-size threshold to avoid false invalidations.
- Anytime-valid methods control false-alarm rate but can be slower to detect small changes; tune power against harm tier.
- The silent-provider-update gap cannot be fully closed for API-served models by any technique short of digest-pinned weights; TTL + fingerprinting + fail-closed is mitigation, not elimination.
